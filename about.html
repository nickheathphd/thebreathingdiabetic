About

Nick Heath, Ph.D.

Author of The Anxious Person's Breath Manual  ·  The Breathing Diabetic

I study breathing at the intersection of physiology, neuroscience, and practical life — not as a wellness trend, but as a measurable physiological process with real consequences for how you feel, think, and function.

I came to this through Type 1 diabetes. Living with the disease makes you acutely aware that stress isn't just mental — it shows up in blood sugar, in sleep, in the body's baseline state. That sent me looking for practical tools to influence the nervous system. What I kept finding was breathing: not magic, but one of the most direct levers we have for shifting how the body and brain actually feel.

How I Got Here

It started with Type 1 diabetes

Living with diabetes makes you acutely aware of stress. Blood sugar isn't influenced only by food and insulin — it's also shaped by the nervous system and emotional state. That connection sent me looking for practical tools.

I began experimenting with structured breathing and found it helped me manage both the physiological and psychological weight of the disease. That led me into the research literature, where I found consistent patterns across study after study.

Slow breathing shifts autonomic balance. It increases heart rate variability. It alters brain activity. And across virtually every study, one of the most reliable outcomes was stress and anxiety reduction.

Over time, it became clear: while breathing influences many systems, the strongest and most consistent evidence is about stress and anxiety regulation.

Why I Focus on Anxiety

It's physiological, not just mental

Stress and anxiety affect essentially everyone — and they affect almost every system in the body. Most approaches focus only on thoughts. But anxiety is also a body problem.

When the nervous system stays activated, the body reinforces the experience even when the mind wants calm. The two systems aren't separate; they're in constant conversation.

Breathing gives us a structured way to interrupt that loop — not as a cure-all, and not as a replacement for therapy, but as a lever. A direct, practical, physiologically grounded one.

My goal isn't to promise that breathing will fix everything. It's to help people understand how their brains and bodies actually work — and give them tools that work with that reality.

What I Believe

The principles behind everything I publish

  • Science should be grounded in real life — not used to make things sound more important than they are.

  • Practices should fit into people's actual schedules — a 5-minute daily habit done consistently beats a complex protocol done twice.

  • Nothing fixes everything. Breathing is a powerful lever — but it's one tool among many, not a miracle cure.

  • Understanding your own physiology changes how you approach your life — not by promising transformation, but by giving you better maps.
